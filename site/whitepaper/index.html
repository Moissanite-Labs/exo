Part I. Overview#

1 The problem#

Language models can now do real work. They write and fix code, draft documents, research questions and run tasks on a computer. Yet working with them day to day has an odd shape. Each session starts from zero. You explain the project, the constraints, the way you like things done. The model does good work. Then the session ends and all of that is gone. Tomorrow you meet a brilliant stranger again.

A few terms first. A model here is a large language model: a program that reads text and predicts the text that should come next. Everything a model knows about your situation must be in its context window, the text sent to it in one request. Windows are measured in tokens, which are pieces of words (in English, a token is about four characters). Anything outside the window does not exist for the model.

Three costs follow from this.

Context is lost. The facts of your work (which repository, which client, what was decided last Tuesday and why) have to be supplied again, by you, or they are missing.

It forgets you. Some products keep a few notes about you inside one vendor's account. Switch model or tool and the notes stay behind. You usually cannot see why a note exists, or fix it when it is wrong.

It does not learn your taste. By taste we mean the many small preferences that make work yours: tone, structure, what counts as done, what must never happen without asking. When you correct a model ("shorter", "lead with the result", "never push without asking me"), the correction lives in that one conversation. Next week you make the same correction again.

Why this matters: what stops people from handing real work to models is more often missing context than missing intelligence. A capable assistant that knows nothing about you can only do generic work, and generic work needs your attention to become your work. Attention is the scarce resource. A system that needs you to re-explain, re-correct and approve every step uses up the very thing it was meant to save.

2 Why the obvious fixes fall short#

Bigger context windows. Windows have grown to around a million tokens. That helps, but a window is not a memory. Everything in it is sent, and paid for, on every request. Very long transcripts also tend to blur a model's focus as they grow, a problem practitioners call context rot. And when the session ends, the window is empty again.

Letting the model keep notes. Saved notes help, but a note with no evidence behind it cannot answer the question "why do you believe this?" A wrong note is applied again and again, and nothing checks it.

Training a model per person. Changing a model's weights for one user is slow and costly, and it ties your history to one model that will be replaced within months.

Exo combines a handful of ideas instead. None is exotic on its own. The rest of Part I builds them up one at a time.

3 Start from a record, not a memory#

Think of how a bank keeps accounts. It does not treat your balance as the truth. It keeps every transaction, and the balance is computed from them. If the balance is ever in doubt, you add up the transactions again.

Exo does the same with your work. Every meaningful event is written once to an append-only ledger: what you said, a standing rule you gave, an action an agent took, and whether you accepted, edited or rejected what it produced. Append-only means entries are added and never changed or deleted. A mistake is fixed by a new entry that refers to the old one.

Everything else Exo knows about you (your profile, its beliefs about you, your skills, the search indexes) is a view: something computed from the ledger that can be thrown away and rebuilt. When a better model arrives next year, it can catch up by replaying the record, the way a new accountant can rebuild the books from the transaction list.

Two protections make the record trustworthy.

  • Each entry is signed. A digital signature is a short code made with a secret key. Anyone with the matching public key can check it, and nobody without the secret key can forge it. So each entry proves who wrote it.
  • Each entry carries the hash of the entry before it. A hash is a fingerprint of data: a short string computed from the data that changes completely if a single character changes. Because each entry contains the fingerprint of the previous one, the entries form a chain. Altering or removing any entry breaks every link after it, and a check finds the break.

4 A store that knows you#

The ledger is complete, but it is far too large and raw to show a model. So Exo keeps a principal store: one folder that holds what the system knows about one person. That person is the principal, the one the system works for. The store has layers, from small and always loaded to large and fetched on demand:

  • The pack. One page, read on every request: who you are, your standing rules, your voice, what needs your approval.
  • Beliefs. Short notes, each about one claim or a few related claims, with a confidence level, the dates when it holds, and links to the evidence. For example: "prefers a table over bullets when comparing options (high confidence, from four linked corrections)."
  • Items. Things you saved elsewhere, such as bookmarks, notes and starred repositories. They are evidence, not beliefs.
  • The ledger, at the bottom.

When a request arrives, Exo must choose which beliefs and items to show the model, because the window is limited and every token costs money and focus. Choosing is called retrieval. Exo uses two kinds of search and merges them. Keyword search ranks notes by the words they share with the request and gives more weight to rare words, since a shared rare word says more than a shared common one. Embedding search turns each note into a list of numbers, called an embedding, arranged so that texts with similar meaning sit near each other, and then finds the notes nearest the request. It finds "keeps replies short" for a request about "brevity" even though no word matches. After merging the two lists, Exo follows links from the best notes to related ones, then packs the result under a fixed token budget.

The embedding model is small and runs on your own machine, so your text does not leave it to be indexed. Keyword retrieval over the store is built. The embedding and link-following stages are designed, not built.

5 One continuous memory#

The store holds what Exo knows about you. The work itself has a different problem. Most assistants keep appending to a growing transcript and send all of it on each request. When it gets too long they compress it once, in a hurry, and quality drifts. And each new chat window starts a new, empty transcript.

Exo builds every turn as a fresh request. Picture a long book with summaries at several levels: a line for each chapter, a paragraph for each part. To keep reading well you need the summaries of everything so far, plus the last few pages in full. Exo keeps exactly that. The most recent messages go to the model word for word. This is the verbatim tail. Older messages are folded into a summary tree. Every eight older messages get one short summary, called a leaf. When the summaries together grow too long, two neighbouring summaries merge into one at the next level up, and so on, so the oldest material is the most compressed.

There is one book per agent, not one per window. An agent here is one configured Exo assistant, called a seat. Every window you open on a seat, every scheduled run and every message from another agent is a thread of that one agent. All threads write to one append-only log and share one summary tree. A thread only changes focus: it brings its own recent messages, plus a short note of what the other threads did since it last looked. So a fact you tell the agent in one window is known in a second window on its first turn. Opening a new window starts a new topic, not a new mind. A thread can be marked private, and then it is logged but never shared.

Before Exo builds the request for a new turn, it waits for any pending summaries to finish, so the view always covers everything that came before. Every original message stays in a local database with full-text search, so a detail that was summarized away can always be found again.

Summaries lose detail by design. The one thing they must never lose is what you told the agent. So capture of your words is a mechanism, not a habit the model is asked to keep. The summary format has a section for your instructions and corrections, quoted word for word. After each summary is written, the harness checks it against your own messages and puts back any ruling the model dropped. And before a stretch of your messages is summarized, it is handed to the memory plugin, which writes your rulings and corrections to the signed ledger. All of this is built.

6 Learning your taste, and proving it#

The most useful signal you can give an assistant is a correction on its own work. If it drafted X and you changed it to Y, you have shown, at the exact point where it went wrong, what you wanted instead. Research on imitation learning found the same thing in robots and games: corrections gathered on the learner's own attempts teach far more than watching an expert work alone.

Exo writes these signals to the ledger: corrections, edits, accepts, rejections, explicit rules, and outcomes such as "the tests passed" or "the post went out". In the background, a reviewer turns them into proposals: small typed lessons with evidence, written as a positive claim. For example: "The principal wants the result first and the detail after, not a long preamble, because they cut the preamble in three drafts this week (entries 41, 77, 102)." The reviewer can only propose. It cannot edit your profile.

A lesson can sound right and still be wrong. So Exo tests lessons before keeping them. It keeps a personal exam built from your own past decisions: questions about you with known answers, pairs of a reply you rejected and the one you chose, and real past moments cut off right before you gave your verdict. A change is scored against the current setup on this exam.

This method is called hill climbing. Picture standing on a hillside in thick fog. You cannot see the top, but you can tell whether a step takes you higher. Take one small step. If you are higher, stay. If not, step back and try another direction. Exo's height is the exam score, and each step is one change you can read, such as one new taste rule.

Any exam has a weak spot: you can learn the answers instead of the subject. A student who memorizes last year's practice paper scores well on it and badly on the real exam. So the newest part of your exam is held out. The learning process never reads it, and a change is kept only if it also does not score worse there. That is the guard against a system that flatters its own test.

This loop is built: capture, the background reviewer, typed proposals, promotion rules, revert, and a nightly climb that keeps a change only if it holds up on held-out cases. What is not yet built is the trigger that runs the climb every night by itself.

7 Acting safely without asking about everything#

An assistant that asks before every step is useless. People cannot watch a quiet system all day. They start approving by reflex, which is worse than not asking. An assistant that never asks is dangerous.

Exo sorts actions by consequence, not by count. It asks whether the action can be undone, who can see it, whether it costs money, and whether it affects someone else. Editing a file on your machine runs at once and is logged. Pushing to a public repository, posting, paying, or sending an email to another person waits for you, with a one-line summary and a sensible default. Some rules protect other people, and your own settings cannot lower them. Anything that touches credentials, such as a login, a key file or a password store, always asks you, and no rule can change that.

Asking is only half of the job. An approval is consent, and consent has to be about something exact. So for anything that leaves your machine, Exo takes a fingerprint (a hash) of the exact text, target and arguments, and your "yes" covers that fingerprint only. If the agent changes one character after you approved, the fingerprint changes and it asks again. "Always allow" is not a blank cheque either. It covers one kind of act on one target, and for an outward act one exact text. It expires after days, not never, it is written to the ledger, and you can list and revoke it. Only you can grant it.

Who may approve is also explicit. By default only you can. You may name another agent as a reviewer for a kind of act, for example public posts, and then that agent's answer must carry its own signature, so an answer cannot be forged or replayed for a different text. A message from another agent is input, never consent: if it tries to approve something, it is refused and shown to you. Exo knows an answer is yours by the connection it came from, your window or your web page, never by what the answer claims, so a command the agent runs cannot approve the agent's own request. And before a post goes to a public place, Exo sweeps it for internal names, home folder paths, model names and internal ids, and holds it if any appear. A false alarm costs one extra question. A leaked name in a public repository cannot be taken back.

Many small choices in this process (which tool, whether a panel should open, which model fits a task) do not need a large model at all. A decision model answers a fixed question with probabilities over fixed options instead of writing text. It is fast and cheap. A probability lets Exo act when it is sure and ask when it is not. One rule is fixed: a decision model may make a gate stricter, never looser. The gate, its approvals and its sweep are built, and the gate already asks a decision model for a second opinion that can only tighten. Calibrating those probabilities on your own decisions is designed, not built.

8 One core, many screens#

Exo's working parts run as one background program, called exod. (A daemon is a program that runs in the background and waits for requests. The "d" in exod is for daemon.) Every screen is a client of it: a terminal, a web page, a chat channel, and one day a small device. Clients speak a small typed protocol: they send commands in, and receive events out.

Every event gets a number, and every event is saved before it is sent. A client that drops off, such as a phone on a bad connection, comes back and asks for everything after the last number it saw. It misses nothing, and two clients watching the same session see exactly the same sequence. This part is built.

The screen itself feels like a terminal: a prompt and a transcript, with three prefix keys and one command palette. Richer elements, such as a diff viewer, an approval card or a task board, are panels that the model opens when they help, and that each client draws as well as it can. A terminal client, a web client and a plain line client are built.

Your keyboard is not the only way in. Another agent may send a message. A long shell command may finish while the agent does other work. Such inputs enter through one door, and the door asks the same three questions every time: who sent this, may it in (yes, hold for you, or no), and should it reach a running turn now or wait for the next one. Then it leaves a receipt. One door means one place to get the rules right. The first two sources through it, messages between agents and the end notices of background shell jobs, are built. Chat channels and scheduled runs are meant to move onto the same door later.

The agent can also hand work to helpers. A helper, or subagent, is a child thread on the same core with a narrower set of tools and hard limits on depth, time and tokens. Each helper runs on the cheapest model tier that can do its job: a small model for searching and reading, a mid-size one for building, the largest only for judgment. A helper that fails gets one retry a tier up. This is built.

9 A harness that watches itself#

A tool that cannot see its own faults cannot fix them. Early on, an Exo agent was asked to judge its own working environment, and it missed the worst problems. One turn took more than eight minutes, and nothing said why. Nothing recorded which model had actually answered, or that a fallback had happened. Cost was always blank. The agent could only see its conversation, not the machinery under it.

So Exo records its own behaviour as data, reads it, reports on it, and checks its own fixes. Every model call leaves an event: which model was asked for, which one answered, each failed try, the wait before the first word, the tokens and the cost. Every turn leaves a timing summary. Every caught error leaves an event, so no failure is silent. A set of plain rules reads these records at the end of each turn, with no model call, and files a finding when something is wrong: a slow turn, a fallback, a tool error, the same command run twice, a shown model that differs from the one that served.

A finding has a stable name, so the same problem counts up instead of piling up. When a developer marks a finding fixed in a given version of the code, Exo keeps watching. If the problem comes back on that version or a later one, the finding reopens. If it stays quiet for 20 turns or 3 days, it is marked verified. A fix is a claim, and the trace is the test of it.

Cost follows one rule of honesty: a price Exo does not know is shown as unknown, never as zero. A zero would look like a free call and hide real spend. All of this is built.

10 Fit one person, work for anyone#

Exo holds two goals at once. It should fit one person as closely as possible, because an assistant that does not really know you is not worth much. And it should work for anyone, because a system built around one person's accounts and habits cannot be handed to a second.

The design answers both with one split. The mechanism (the code: the daemon, the ledger, the store format, retrieval, the learning loop) is generic and ships to everyone. The principal (one person's data, profile, rules and tuning) lives only in that person's store. No line of the core may name a user, an account or a machine, and a test checks this. A fresh install with an empty store runs end to end, and an onboarding interview builds a new person's store from zero. Updating the core must never lose anything in a store.

That is the whole system at a high level: a signed record at the bottom, a portable store of what it knows about you, one continuous memory with fresh and complete context on every turn, lessons that are kept only if they prove themselves, gates by consequence with consent bound to exact text, one core and one way in behind every screen, and a harness that measures itself. Part II covers each piece in depth.

Part II. Technical design#

11 Architecture at a glance#

The core is a Python package. Its runtime dependencies are two libraries: PyNaCl (signatures) and httpx (HTTP to model providers). The HTTP server inside exod is written on the standard library's asyncio, so it adds no web framework. Storage is SQLite files and plain Markdown files.

The core has a line budget. A test counts the lines in src/exo/core, src/exo/protocol, src/exo/server and two command-line modules. Wave 1 shipped under 8,000. The plan allowed 15,000 by the end of wave 3, and the test now fails above that. Today the count is 14,694, so the next large addition must either remove something or argue for a higher budget. The budget exists so that growth is a decision, and so the core can later shrink onto a small device (section 27).

12 The core daemon and its event protocol#

12.1 Why a daemon#

One process owns all state that matters: sessions, running turns, tools, plugins. Clients render and send input. This split has three effects. Every client gets the same behaviour (retries, routing, gates, compaction) without reimplementing it. A client can crash or close without losing work. And a new kind of client, such as a device with a two-line screen or no screen, needs only to speak the protocol.

12.2 Commands, responses, events#

The protocol has three envelopes, all JSON, all defined once as Python dataclasses in src/exo/protocol/schema.py. A script generates JSON Schema and TypeScript types from them, so clients in other languages agree with the server by construction.

  • Command: {id, type, body}. A client asks for something.
  • Response: {id, ok, result, error}. The direct answer to one command, matched by id.
  • Event: {id, seq, session, type, ts, body}. Something that happened. seq is the event's number.

Decoding is strict. An unknown command type fails with unknown_command. An unknown or missing body field fails with bad_body. Clients may decode events loosely, dropping fields they do not know, so an older client keeps working against a newer server. The handshake (hello) carries protocol: 1. Within a version, changes are additive only.

The surface is small on purpose. A registry decorator refuses to register more than 30 command types or 30 event types, and tests enforce it. For contrast, the terminal gateway of the harness Exo learned most from has 257 methods. When a feature needs more, it adds fields or event types, not one-off methods. The cap is now tight, and it has shaped the design: peers got one command with four actions instead of four commands, and features such as /trace, /context, /compact, /handoff and /grants are named commands run through the generic command.run, with no new protocol type.

12.3 Ordering, durability and replay#

A single hub inside the server numbers every event. It takes the next seq, writes the event to the events table in sessions.sqlite, and only then hands it to live subscribers. Three properties follow:

  1. seq rises strictly across the whole stream, and events of one session stay in order.
  2. Nothing is ever shown that was not first saved. A crash cannot produce an event a client saw but the record lacks.
  3. Replay is a database read. events.subscribe with since=N returns every event numbered above N, then continues live. Over HTTP, the standard Last-Event-ID header does the same, so a browser's built-in reconnect resumes in place.

The acceptance test for wave 1 checks this end to end. It runs a three-turn session through exod and the line client (read a file, edit it, run a shell command), kills the server with SIGKILL in the middle, restarts it, resumes the session, and then confirms that the numbering is dense across the restart, that two clients replaying from zero receive byte-identical events, and that the database rows match what clients saw.

12.4 Transports#

One dispatcher, three ways in:

  • HTTP with Server-Sent Events over a Unix socket (the default). POST /command carries a command and returns a response. GET /events?since=N&session=S is a Server-Sent Events (SSE) stream: one long HTTP response that the server keeps writing events into. A comment line every 15 seconds keeps idle connections alive. GET /health reports the protocol version and the last seq.
  • The same over TCP, for other devices on the same machine or network.
  • JSON lines over standard input and output, so a client can start the core as a child process and talk to it through a pipe.

The schema does not change between transports. A remote transport over the Exo bus or a tunnel is planned and uses the same envelopes designed, not built.

12.5 Security of the socket and the token#

  • Unix socket. A Unix socket is a file-like endpoint on the local disk. The server creates its directory with owner-only permissions, binds the socket under a restrictive file-creation mask so there is no moment when it is open to others, and then sets it to mode 0600. Only the same operating-system user can connect, so the socket needs no token.
  • TCP. The server refuses to start a TCP listener without a bearer token. The token is 32 random bytes, URL-safe encoded, written to a file created exclusively with mode 0600, or taken from an environment variable. Every HTTP request must carry Authorization: Bearer <token>. The comparison is constant-time (hmac.compare_digest) so response timing does not leak how much of a guess was right.
  • Input limits. Command bodies over 8 MB are refused. Malformed input gets a typed error, never a crash. An exception inside the engine becomes an internal error response and the connection stays up.
  • What is missing. The TCP transport has no TLS today. It is meant for localhost or a trusted local network. Encrypted remote access goes through the bus or a tunnel, which is not built yet.

12.6 The engine behind the dispatcher#

The server talks to an Engine interface with one method, handle(command, hub). Long work, such as a turn, starts in the background, returns quickly ({"accepted": true}), and reports progress as events. The real engine (exo.core.engine) wires together the router, tool registry, loop, session store and plugins. A tiny echo engine exists for protocol tests.

12.7 One way in: ingress and peers#

Anything that does not come from the principal's own keyboard passes through one path, the ingress. It takes four facts (source, sender, trust, body) and runs three steps:

  1. Policy. The receiving seat's gate policy answers auto, hold or deny. A held message waits for an approval card for up to 30 minutes and then expires. A held message never enters the model's context.
  2. Delivery. If a turn is running, the message is steered in at the next tool round. If the thread is idle, it is queued for the next turn. It wakes the thread (starts a turn by itself) only if the seat allows it, and by default it does not while a person has a client attached.
  3. Record. A receipt goes back to the sender (delivered, held, released, denied or expired), and an event and a trace row record what happened.

The first source is peers: live Exo sessions on one machine that message each other. The idea comes from Claude Code, where one running session can tell another what it is doing, for example to avoid editing the same file. Each running exod writes a small presence file (owner-only, removed on exit). A message is an envelope signed with the sending seat's key, and it names sender, target and a one-time number inside the signed part, so the receiver can check the signature, the age and that it is not a replay.

13 The model router#

13.1 Providers and transports#

Exo talks to models through two transports, lifted from Hermes Agent and trimmed: the Anthropic Messages API, and the OpenAI-compatible chat API. The second covers OpenAI, OpenRouter and local servers such as llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio and vLLM. Both stream. Both turn the provider's stream into one internal event shape: text and reasoning deltas, whole tool_calls (the transport assembles argument fragments), usage, and exactly one final done or error. An error carries the HTTP status, a retryable flag and any retry_after the provider gave.

Which providers exist, their base URLs, and which environment variable holds each key are data, in catalog.toml, not code. A provider may list several key variables. Each becomes a separate credential that the router can rotate through.

A third kind of provider is a vendor's own command-line tool, run as a subprocess (claude-cli, muse-cli). It exists for subscriptions that can only be used through the vendor's login. The login token stays with the vendor's program, in the OS keychain, and Exo never touches it. The price is honest: one process per turn, no token stream, and for muse-cli no tool calls, so the router skips it when a turn needs tools.

13.2 Roles#

Code never asks for a model by name. It asks for a role:

  • main: the model that runs the conversation.
  • aux: cheaper side work, such as summaries and titles.
  • decision: small structured choices (section 14).
  • eval: the judge in the learning loop. Empty by default, which means the main route.
  • fast, standard, deep: three tiers, cheapest to most capable, used for subagents and scheduled runs (section 16.1). The Anthropic defaults are Haiku, Sonnet and Opus.

Each role is an ordered list of routes, written provider:model. If a role is empty or every route fails, resolution falls back: decision to aux to main, aux to main, and each tier toward main. A tier whose provider has no key is skipped, so a setup with one local model runs everything on it and nothing breaks. Exo is provider agnostic: every role is the user's choice, the user's config overrides the catalog, and models.set changes a role at run time. Keys come from environment variables, then the operating system's keychain, then one owner-only key file, and never from chat.

13.3 Fallback#

For each route in the chain, the router picks the first credential that is not cooling down, and streams. Two rules govern what happens on failure:

  1. Fall back only before output starts. If the provider fails before sending any text, reasoning or tool call, the router quietly tries the next credential, then the next route. Once output has started, the error goes to the loop, because silently switching models in the middle of a reply would splice two different answers.
  2. Do not fall back on a bad request. HTTP 400, 413 and 422 mean the request itself is wrong (malformed, too large). Another route would fail the same way, so the error goes straight back.

When everything is exhausted, the router returns one error that says why, and when the next route reopens.

13.4 Cooldown#

A failed credential is set aside for a while. How long depends on the failure:

FailureCooldown

The last row needs care. When a provider says "try again in 6 hours" and you have only one key, waiting the full 6 hours is often wrong: limits are frequently lifted early. So a lone credential is probed again after max(60 s, 2% of the remaining window), never later than the stated reset, and each failed probe re-stamps a shorter window. This rule came from a fix contributed to Hermes Agent and was written into Exo's router from the start.

13.5 Cost#

The catalog carries list prices per million tokens for known models, and the user's config can add or override a price. When a provider reports token usage without a cost, the router computes one from the price of the route that actually served, counting cache reads and cache writes at their own rates. Some providers count cached tokens inside their input total, so only the uncached part is charged at the input rate. An early version charged those tokens twice. Usage flows to clients as usage.updated events with a running session total, and each call's cost goes into the trace (section 23).

A route with no known price costs unknown, never $0. A turn with some unpriced calls shows the sum of the priced ones followed by a plus sign. Subscription plans with no per-token price read unknown until the user sets one. The reason is simple: a zero looks like a free call, and a wrong zero hides spend that is real.

14 Decision models#

Status. The decision role, the typed probability-returning interface, the Jev and Laya providers and the gate's tighten-only use of them are built. Calibration, decision ledger entries, panel and routing decisions, and exo-decide are designed, not built.

14.1 What a decision model is#

Many choices inside a harness have a fixed set of answers. Which of these 12 tools does this turn need? Should a panel open now, and which one? Is this message a correction? Is this action public? A generative model can answer such questions by writing text, but it is slow and costly for the job, and its answer comes with no usable measure of how sure it is.

A decision model takes a short text (the "state") and typed questions, and returns probabilities instead of prose. Three question types are shared by the products available today: choice (pick one of N options), score (a level on an ordered scale), and a yes-or-no predicate. It does not write text, so it never fills in tool arguments. It picks which tool, which panel, which label. A generative model still writes the arguments, drafts and plans.

14.2 Where they fit#

The rule of thumb: if the answer is a pick from a known list, a yes or no, or a level, a decision model fits. If the answer is text, it does not. Exact rules stay in code.

MechanismFitsQuestion shape

14.3 Tighten only#

A decision model may move an action toward "ask", never toward "allow". Rules in code run first, and anything guarded (irreversible, public, financial, third-party) never depends on a model's answer. This keeps a miscalibrated or manipulated model from opening a door that code had closed.

14.4 Calibration, fallback and logging#

Each vendor computes "confidence" with a different formula, and one independent test of a hosted product found it right far less often than its stated confidence implied. So Exo plans to gate on the probability of the chosen answer after per-provider, per-mechanism calibration: fitting a correction on the user's own labelled decisions so that "0.9" really means right about 9 times in 10. The raw vendor number is kept in the log but not used.

Each call runs in this order. Rules come first. Then providers are tried in the user's order, each with a short timeout (planned defaults: 0.5 s local, 2 s hosted). A timeout, rate limit or network error moves to the next provider. Below the threshold, Exo escalates to the main model, to the person, or to a safe default (no panel, keep the current model, ask before acting). Abstaining is a normal outcome. The turn never blocks on a decision model.

Every decision is planned as a decision ledger entry holding hashes and probabilities, not raw text, so the signed ledger can travel without carrying private content. Later signals that refer to it (the person opened a different panel, undid the action, accepted the result) become its labels. That is what lets the learning loop and the eval score each mechanism and each provider on the user's own data.

14.5 Defaults: Jev first, Laya local#

The first plan made Laya the default: an open-weight model (Apache 2.0) small enough to run on a laptop, free and private. A live check changed that. Asked "Is deleting a production database reversible?", base Laya said yes with probability 0.62. TypeSafe's Jev said no with 0.80. Laya's own server also warns that its confidence is not calibrated, and its model card reports 0.362 against a random baseline of 0.318 on its typed-decisions set without fine-tuning. A gate cannot lean on that.

So the default decision route is now Jev, then local Laya, then aux, then main. Jev is a hosted service, so it is used only when the user has set a TypeSafe key. Without one, the role falls through to Laya, and without Laya to the chat models, which answer the same typed question as JSON. A hosted decision model sees the state it is asked about.

Laya can take the default back. Exo plans to ship a fine-tuned version, "exo-decide", trained on a generic seed set and, per user, on that user's own verdicts. It becomes the default only if it beats Jev on a held-out set. Licence terms shape the data: TypeSafe's terms forbid training other models on its outputs, so those outputs are never training labels. Weights stay private until they beat base Laya and Jev on a held-out set. None of this is measured yet.

15.1 The registry#

A tool is a ToolSpec (name, description, JSON Schema for its arguments, a risk hint of low, medium or high, and a toolset name) plus a handler and an optional availability check (for example, "is this binary on the PATH?"). Toolsets let a session or a subagent narrow what the model sees. The registry never raises for a tool failure: it returns a result marked as an error, so the model sees what went wrong and the turn continues. Results over 100,000 characters are cut. Handlers that block run in a worker thread so a slow shell command never stalls the server.

code_find exists because "where is X decided?" costs an agent many rounds of search and read. On four questions about Exo's own repository it found the right files each time with about half the input tokens of a small subagent using grep and read, but it was slower and no more correct. A native version waits for a measured win on larger, messier code.

15.2 Hooks#

Named hook points let plugins watch and steer. The names follow Hermes so existing plugins keep working. The engine fires these today built:

  • pre_tool_call: may return block (the model receives the reason as an error result) or approve (ask the person first).
  • post_approval_response: told what the person chose.
  • post_tool_call: sees the result.
  • transform_tool_result: may rewrite the result text.

More names are reserved and validated (for model calls, turns, sessions, compaction, commands, subagents and skills) but not yet fired by the loop. A failing hook is logged and skipped, so one broken plugin never breaks a turn. A block decision is still honoured.

15.3 The approval flow#

When a hook asks for approval, the engine publishes approval.requested with a request id, a summary, the risk, the tool call, the list of who may answer (approvers) and a binding hash, and waits. A client answers with approval.respond: allow, deny or allow_always. The engine publishes approval.resolved and continues. Because the question is an event, a terminal, a web page, a phone or a device can answer it, whichever the person has at hand. Four rules make the answer mean what it says:

  1. Bound to exact text. For an outward act (a post, message, email, payment, account change, or anything public or irreversible) the gate hashes the exact text, title, target, tool and arguments. That hash is the binding. An answer covers that hash only, so an edit of one character is a new ask. A client may echo the binding. If it differs from the open request, the answer is refused as stale, so a person who read an older draft cannot pass a newer one. Hashing all the arguments, not only the text, means nothing else can change under an approval either.
  2. Who may answer. A policy file maps each kind of act to principal, a named seat, or a role that lists seats. The default is the principal only. The principal can always answer, because their consent is the root. Anyone else is refused as forbidden.
  3. Signed seat answers. A seat answers as itself and must sign the answer with its Ed25519 key. The signature covers the request id, the decision, the binding hash and the seat name, so it cannot be reused for another request, decision or text. An unsigned seat answer is refused. A principal answer is not signed: it is as strong as the socket's file permissions, which admit only the same operating-system user.
  4. Scoped, expiring grants. allow_always becomes a grant. Its scope is the kind of act and the exact target, and for an outward act also the exact text hash, so one approval can never cover a later, different text. It expires (30 days by default, 7 for outward acts), each use is logged, and creating or revoking it writes a ledger row. Grants live in the principal store, not in the daemon's memory, so they survive a restart and hold for every seat of that principal. Only the principal can create one: a seat's allow_always is refused. /grants lists them and /grants revoke removes one. Credentials never get a grant.

15.4 The gate#

The gate (exo.gate, about 1,300 lines, stdlib only) is two pure steps. classify looks at a tool name and its arguments and places the action on four axes: reversibility, exposure, cost, and effect on third parties. It parses shell commands into their parts (pipes, &&, heredocs, sudo and env prefixes) and knows the common cases, for example that git push to a shared remote is public, that gh pr comment writes where others read, and that an HTTP POST to a non-local host leaves the machine. decide then maps that cell through the person's policies: a gate matrix, scopes, overrides, reserved and denied actions, budget and posting checks. The outcome is allow, log, ask or deny.

Under the person's policy sit two floors that no policy, rule, grant or scope can lower. The operator floor: an irreversible public act that touches a third party always asks. Amplifying a person is neutral. It would amplify a bad actor too, so rules that protect other people sit outside the model of the person. The credentials floor: logins, key files, password stores and anything else that handles secrets always ask the principal. After the rules, the gate asks the decision model whether the act is riskier than the rules thought, and may only move the outcome toward ask (section 14.3).

The last check is the outbound scrub. A post to a public target is swept for internal identifiers: seat and agent names, home folder paths, model ids from the catalog, internal ids of sessions, tasks and jobs, and any names the user lists. A hit holds the post and lists each one. A plain one-word name matches only as a whole word, so a seat called "scout" does not hold every post that mentions a scout. A distinctive name, with a hyphen, underscore or digit, also matches inside longer words and with any separator. The sweep leans toward holding, because the costs are lopsided: a false hit costs one question, a miss leaks a name for good. The rule came from a real slip, when a public repository shipped with task ids, profile names, model strings and a home path in it.

The gate is built and tested as a library and as the exo-gate plugin, which runs it on every tool call on the core and, when a principal store with a signing key is attached, writes each decision to the signed ledger.

15.5 Background jobs#

A build or a long test run should not hold a turn. With background: true, bash returns a job id at once and the agent carries on. Output goes to an owner-only log file that rotates at 5 MB. A job belongs to the agent, not to the turn: stopping the turn (ctrl+c or /stop) never stops the job. When the job ends, the core writes a short notice (exit code, time, the last lines of output, where the full log is) and sends it through the ingress path (section 12.7). It is steered into a running turn, queued for an idle one, or wakes the thread if the seat allows. The notice is framed as data from the harness. It is never the user, never counts as the principal's words, and cannot approve anything.

Limits are per agent: 4 jobs at once, 2 hours of wall time, 5 MB of output. Jobs end with exod. If exod dies without closing, the next start reports the jobs as lost and leaves the processes alone, because an operating system can reuse a process id, and killing by a stale id could hit an unrelated program. A background command is gated exactly like a foreground one, and a test compares the two. Subagents cannot start jobs, since their threads end with their report.

16 The agent loop#

exo.core.loop runs one turn as a short series of steps. Everything it needs (the model, the tool runner, the memory provider, the context engine) is injected, so the loop imports none of them and each can be tested alone.

  1. Close any tool call left dangling by a crash with an error result, so the transcript stays valid.
  2. Store the user message and emit message.completed, then turn.started.
  3. Build the system message: the base prompt, the memory provider's pack block and the agent's summary tree. Memory recalled for this message goes in a separate message just before it, not in the system message (section 17.1 explains why).
  4. Build a fresh model input from the context engine (section 17).
  5. Stream one model call. Text arrives as message.delta, reasoning as reasoning.delta. Usage arrives as usage.updated, and a model.call event records the route asked for, the route that served, every attempt and the timings (section 23).
  6. Store the reply (message.completed). If it asked for tools, run them in order, each with tool.started, any tool.output, then tool.completed, and store all results as one tool message. Go back to step 4.
  7. When the model stops asking for tools, emit turn.completed with the stop reason, usage and a timing summary, tell the memory provider about the exchange, and emit session.updated.

Limits and edge cases:

  • Iteration cap. At most 24 tool rounds per turn by default. After that, the loop emits a notice and stops.
  • Retries. A retryable model error is retried up to 3 times with exponential backoff, but only while nothing has been shown. Once text has streamed, retrying would show the person a different answer on top of the first.
  • Interrupt. turn.interrupt stops the stream or the running tool. Partial text is kept, marked as interrupted. Every tool call still gets a result ("interrupted before completion"), so the transcript the model sees next time is well formed.
  • Steer and queue. While a turn runs, turn.send with mode: steer slips the new message in between tool rounds. A steer that lands while the final reply is being written gets its own model call in the same turn, so it is never dropped. queue waits for the turn to end.
  • Fail soft. A memory provider or context plugin that throws is ignored for that call, and a harness.error event records it. An unexpected error inside the loop ends the turn with error and a clean turn.completed, never a hung session.

16.1 Subagents and the agent wire#

A subagent is a child thread on the same core, started by the delegate tool with one goal or a list of tasks. It runs on the same router, tools and gate, so every approval and hook applies to a child's calls exactly as to the parent's. A child never gains a tool its parent lacks, and tools that need a person (ask, clarify, send a message, schedule a job, open a panel) are blocked for it. Limits, by default: one level of nesting, 10 tasks per call, 4 children at once per level, 24 tool rounds, 200,000 tokens and 15 minutes per child. Each level has its own pool of slots, so a parent waiting on its children can never starve them. Interrupting the parent cancels every child below it. The parent gets back a receipt in plain English: outcome, what was done, the model that ran it, and why it failed if it did.

Cheapest model that can do the job. Each child gets a tier: a scout (reading, searching) runs fast, a builder or verifier standard, a critic deep. The parent may set a tier per task. A child never runs above its parent's tier unless the user allows it. A child that fails is retried once a tier up, as a separate task with its own receipt. Most delegated work is retrieval and simple building, which a small model does well at a fraction of the price. Judgment keeps its tier, and a failure buys one stronger try.

Lines for machines, English for models. Briefs and receipts are stored as compact typed lines in the SYM-2P format: one packet per line, fields by position, small numeric codes for roles, acts and states, and a nested symbolic body. Code writes, checks, signs and replays these lines. Models never read or write them. A fixed renderer turns each packet into the same English every time, and that is what a model or a person reads. The reason is a measurement with real tokenizers. Lines save 30 to 48% of bytes against JSON, but not model tokens: a receipt is 121 tokens as a line and 83 as English, and a model would also need a 193-token key to read lines at all. So the wire's value is not fewer tokens. It is that every brief and receipt is a typed packet that validates strictly, carries limits the harness enforces, can be signed, and replays exactly.

17 Context and the agent's memory#

17.1 A fresh call every turn, one memory per agent#

Exo does not keep a growing transcript. Every model call is built new from older history made of summaries and the verbatim tail of recent messages. This idea comes from the OptChat design and its production form, UniiChat (section 33). Exo reimplements the idea. It copies no code or prompt text from either.

The unit of memory is the agent, not the window. A seat is one agent with one append-only log and one summary tree. Windows, channels, scheduled runs and peer messages are threads of it. Each message in a thread also takes the next position in the agent's log, and each log entry stores a hash over the previous entry's hash and its own content, so exo memory verify can check the log end to end. Undo hides entries instead of deleting them, so the log stays append-only.

A model call for one thread has four parts, in this order:

  1. System message. The base prompt, the memory provider's block, then the agent's summary tree across all threads, oldest first. It ends at the last finished summary, so it is byte-identical for every thread of the agent until a new summary lands.
  2. This thread's tail. Every message of the thread after the tree's frontier, and at least its last 12.
  3. Notes from other threads. Before a call, what other threads did since this thread last looked is written into this thread as one compact note ("In thread "tui 2", 4 min ago: ..."). The note is stored, so the next call sends the same bytes. A new thread's first note covers everything after the frontier, so a second window knows what the first just did on its first turn.
  4. Recall. What the memory provider recalls for this message, placed just before it and not stored.

The order is about the provider's prompt cache. Providers charge much less for the part of a request that starts with exactly the same bytes as a recent one. So everything that changes rarely goes first, and everything that changes each turn goes last. Recall used to sit in the system message, where it changed every turn and broke the cache for the whole prompt. Providers that key their cache on a conversation id get one id per agent, so every window shares the cached prefix. A thread marked private is logged but never enters the tree, other threads' notes or search. Subagents keep their own transcripts outside the log, and their result reaches the parent as the delegate receipt.

One process writes an agent's log: it holds an operating-system file lock for that agent. A second window for the same seat joins the first window's core instead of starting a second one, and takes over if the first closes. Two threads may run turns at the same time. Log positions are assigned inside one database write, so they never collide, and each thread sees the other's entries at its next call through a note.

17.2 The summary tree#

The rules, with today's defaults built:

  • Log entries older than the tail (the last 12) are cut into leaves of 8. Each leaf is summarized once, and its text labels each entry with its thread.
  • A node at level L covers 2 to the power L leaves. Addresses are (level, index), so a node's children are (L-1, 2i) and (L-1, 2i+1).
  • When the summaries in the view total more than 6,000 tokens (estimated as characters divided by four), the oldest adjacent pair of finished nodes at the same level is merged into its parent.
  • The view shows the coarsest finished nodes that tile the summarized region, oldest first, each labelled with the range it covers. Anything after the last finished node stays in the recent part, word for word, so nothing is ever missing.
  • A span too small to be worth summarizing stays verbatim, and a summary that is not shorter than its source is dropped.
  • The view is introduced to the model as background, not instructions, and its header asks the model to say in its reply anything it learned that will matter later, since summaries keep little tool output.

17.3 Lanes and settling#

Summaries run in parallel in two lanes with separate limits: live chat (8 at once) and imported history (2 at once). A large import, such as a year of past chats, therefore never starves the live conversation, and imports can be paused and resumed. Before building a turn, the context engine settles: it waits, up to 60 seconds, until no live summary is pending. On timeout it proceeds, using verbatim text for the unfinished part. Interrupted summary jobs are retried when the session is next touched, including after a restart. A failed job is retried with backoff, at most three times; then its span stays verbatim and one finding is recorded (D-041). Each finished node is reported as a memory.summarized event. It is upkeep of the long-term memory, not a compaction of the live context, so it is kept apart from context.compacted.

17.4 The summarizer#

The summarizer is any model on the aux role. Its prompt wording is lifted from Hermes Agent's compressor and trimmed. It tells the summarizer that the turns are data to summarize, never instructions to follow. It must replace any secret it sees with a placeholder, keep the user's language, and keep paths, commands, errors and values exact. It must also record the user's latest unfinished request precisely, including a request to stop or undo, so a cancelled task is not carried forward. And it must copy every instruction, correction, preference and decision of the principal word for word into a section of its own, which merges keep whole.

A prompt is a request, not a guarantee, so the harness checks it. After a leaf is summarized, the harness finds the principal's rulings and corrections in that span with the same rules the learning loop uses for capture. Any whose words are missing from the summary are appended under a heading that says the harness restored them. Before a leaf that holds principal messages is summarized, the span is offered to the memory provider's capture_principal. The memory plugin writes the rulings, corrections and open decisions to the signed ledger, each with the agent reply it answers, so a correction keeps its target. The leaf records how many principal messages it held and how many were captured. A principal message is a live message typed by the principal: not a peer message, a job notice, an import, a scheduled prompt or a bare "ok".

Two commands make this visible. /context shows what the next call will carry, part by part, with token estimates, and how many principal messages are not yet captured. /compact captures every uncaptured principal message first, then summarizes everything eligible at once.

17.5 Threads in SQLite#

One sessions.sqlite file per Exo home holds threads (still called sessions in the protocol), messages, the agent log, events, summary nodes, background jobs, findings and a full-text index. It runs in write-ahead-log mode so readers do not block the writer. When the one-memory model arrived, existing sessions were imported into their agent's log once, in time order, and nothing was deleted.

  • Search. Every message's text, tool calls and tool results go into an SQLite FTS5 index. search returns matches with highlighted snippets. Input is quoted, so any text is a safe query.
  • Fork. session.fork copies a session's messages up to a chosen message into a new session and records the parent and fork point, so lineage can be walked back.
  • Resume. session.resume reattaches after a restart: it repairs a half-finished tool round and restarts pending summaries.
  • Undo. session.undo hides the last N turns from the conversation and from search. A summary already made keeps them. It does not roll back files on disk. Snapshot undo of files is not built.

18 Plugins and slots#

A plugin is a folder with a plugin.yaml manifest and a Python register(ctx) function, or an installed package that advertises an exo.plugins entry point. This is the same format Hermes Agent uses, kept on purpose so that Exo's six existing plugins load on the new core with import changes only.

The context object offers register_tool, register_hook, register_command, register_memory_provider, register_context_engine, register_channel and register_skill. If register raises, everything that plugin registered is rolled back and the error is kept on its record. One bad plugin never stops the core.

Two registrations are slots: exclusive seats that only one plugin can hold at a time.

  • Memory provider. Supplies the pack block for the system prompt, recalls memory for each message, hears about each finished exchange, and may add its own tools.
  • Context engine. May reshape the messages built for a turn and hears about usage after each turn.

19.3 Nodes#

Each node has required frontmatter: id, title, type, domain, summary, load_when, confidence (high, medium, low), stability (core, durable, volatile), valid_from, sources and links. Links are typed: part-of, explains, evidence-for, contradicts, supersedes, applies-to, instance-of, see-also, and in version 2 derived-from. A belief that is replaced is not deleted. It gets a valid_until date and a supersedes link from its successor.

exo lint checks a store: missing or mistyped keys, an id that does not match its path, dangling links, a missing pack or router, and leak patterns (phone numbers, API keys, tokens, passwords, private keys) in any curated file. The empty template store lints with zero errors and zero warnings.

19.4 Items#

Items are things the person saved, such as bookmarks or notes designed, not built. Each row has a deterministic id computed from its source (so re-running an import changes nothing), a normalized URL and a content hash for de-duplication, a quality tier (curated, inferred, noise), and a review status. Every insert also appends an item entry to the ledger, so the table can be rebuilt. Items are evidence, not beliefs: the learning loop may propose a belief from many items, and the person reviews it like any other. Conversations are not items. They enter the ledger as utterances, in the person's own words.

20 The signed ledger#

Status. built, as exo.ledger, with tests. On the core, the memory plugin writes each turn's words to it as utterance entries, and the gate writes its decisions and grants, when a principal store with a signing key is attached. The agent log of section 17.1 is a separate, simpler chain: hashed but not signed, and kept with the threads.

Each event is one JSON document in a canonical form (sorted keys, no whitespace, UTF-8), so the same content always produces the same bytes. Its fields:

FieldMeaning
idSHA-256 of the canonical event without id and sig
tsUTC time to the microsecond
authorthe person or agent that wrote it
kindutterance, ruling, verdict, action, artifact, ingest, question, answer, belief_proposed, belief_accepted or note
bodya JSON object
sessionsession id, or null
refsids of earlier events this one refers to (each must already exist)
prevthe chain hash of the previous event (64 zeros for the first)
pub, sigthe signer's Ed25519 public key, and its signature over the SHA-256 of the event without sig

The chain hash of an event is sha256(prev + id). verify() recomputes every id, link, chain hash and signature, and can also pin authors to known keys, so a valid signature by the wrong key is caught.

SQLite triggers abort any UPDATE or DELETE on the events table, so append-only is enforced by the database, not by good manners. Appends run inside BEGIN IMMEDIATE, so several processes can write to one ledger without racing for the chain head. Keys are stored in files with mode 0600.

A verdict is the highest-value kind. Its body names the judged event and one of accept, edit, reject or should_have_been, and the judged event is added to refs. This is the raw material of the learning loop.

Version 2 plans more kinds designed, not built: item, retrieval (which node and item ids went into a prompt, which feeds use counts and evaluation), decision (section 14), and state and lease for shared team state with revision checks, so two writers cannot overwrite each other.

21 Retrieval#

Status. Keyword ranking with a budgeted pack is built (exo.retrieve.assemble, with a Ranker seam). The staged pipeline below is designed, not built.

The stages, explained:

  1. Pack. Always first and prompt-cached (the provider keeps it so it is not re-billed at full price), outside the retrieval budget.
  2. Router pins. Load rules in each INDEX.md whose trigger text matches the query pin their nodes, which enter first and in full. A human-written rule beats any ranking.
  3. Candidates. Two ranked lists. BM25 is a standard keyword ranking: a document scores higher the more query words it contains, rare words count more than common ones, repeating a word has diminishing returns, and long documents are not favoured for being long. Planned column weights: title 3, summary 2, load_when 2, tags 1, body 1. The second list is embedding similarity (section 21.1). The two lists are combined by reciprocal rank fusion: each document's score is the sum, over the lists, of 1 divided by (60 plus its rank in that list). Fusion by rank avoids comparing two scores measured on different scales. Nodes get a 1.5 times prior over items.
  4. Filters. Drop beliefs past their valid_until unless the query is about the past. Drop private nodes for agent scopes. Never return raw private text or archives.
  5. Graph expansion. From the top 8 nodes, walk one link. A supersedes link swaps in the newer node. A contradicts link pulls in the other side, because both views matter. Other links add their target at the parent's score times a weight: 0.5 for explains, evidence-for and applies-to, 0.4 for part-of and instance-of, and 0.3 for see-also.
  6. Pack under budget. Pinned nodes first, then roughly 70% nodes and 30% items of what is left. A node that does not fit in full appears as its summary line. Items degrade from full card to title to bare URL. Every block carries its id, so the model can ask for the full node.
  7. Log a retrieval event with the ids and token count.

21.1 Local embeddings#

The default embedding model is planned to be EmbeddingGemma 300M, which is built for phones and small boards and handles many languages. Qwen3-Embedding 0.6B (Apache 2.0) is the alternative where Gemma's terms are not acceptable or more strength on long text is wanted. Vectors are cut to 256 dimensions (both models are trained so that a prefix of the vector is still a useful vector), normalized, and stored as 8-bit integers: 256 bytes per item, so 100,000 items take about 25 MB. Every vector row records its model, dimensions and content hash, so unchanged text is never embedded twice and a model change triggers a clean rebuild. Search is a flat scan in SQLite. At this scale no vector server is needed. A remote embedding service is possible only as an opt-in that the person's policy must allow, because it sends personal text off the machine.

22 The learning loop#

22.1 Signals#

Every signal is a ledger event. Default strengths:

22.2 The reviewer#

One background reviewer runs after a turn when there is something to learn: a correction, edit or rejection arrived, a rule arrived, ten user turns or ten tool rounds passed, or the person asked for a review. Turns with no new signal skip it, and it never runs for scheduled jobs or subagents. A new live turn cancels it. Its input is the new ledger events since its last run plus the beliefs they touch, not the whole transcript, which keeps it cheap enough for a small model.

It works in two stages. Classify each event. Is it signal or noise? Is it taste, fact, procedure or an open question? Which domain and node does it touch? Does it agree, conflict, or add something new? This stage can run on rules or a small model. Reflect only on survivors: write the lesson as a positive claim with its wider meaning and its evidence. Lessons are written as "wants Y, not X, because Z", never as a stored bad example with a "this was wrong" note, since models tend to learn the example and lose the negation.

Its only write tool is propose. Each lesson goes to exactly one home: taste to the pack, how-to to the governing skill, facts to retrieval with sources, ambiguity to a question. For a proposal that would change a high-confidence belief or a heavily used skill, a short second pass argues against it ("is this a one-off? does it contradict a rule?") and attaches its objection.

22.3 Weight and promotion#

A lesson's weight is a pure function of its evidence, so it can always be recomputed from the ledger. Each piece of evidence has a strength s and an age, and fades with a half-life (30 days for preferences, while rules never fade). The faded strengths combine by noisy-or: weight = 1 minus the product of (1 minus each faded strength). Read it as "the chance that at least one piece of evidence is real". Repeated evidence pushes the weight toward 1, and silence lets it fall. Weight of 0.75 or more is labelled high, and 0.45 or more medium built, in exo.taste.

Promotion, in order:

  1. The person said it (a rule, an answer, or accepting the proposal): promote now.
  2. Guarded (irreversible, public, financial, third-party): never auto-promote. Exo asks or waits.
  3. Conflicts with an existing belief: apply the store's conflict rule (by default the newer wins unless the older one guards something irreversible or public, in which case Exo asks one numbered question). Never overwrite silently.
  4. Taste or fact, unguarded, high weight, evidence from at least two separate sessions, no objection: promote, and show it in the next brief as learned.
  5. Skill changes: only through the eval gate below, because skills change behaviour most.
  6. Everything else waits. A medium-weight lesson may enter the pack as a hint. A stale one becomes a question if asking is worth it, or fades away.

High-weight taste is enforced. Medium is a hint. Low is not shown. Learned taste appears inline in the chat with a link to the plain file that holds it, so the person can edit or delete it.

22.4 Reverting appends#

Lessons are views of the ledger, so undoing one adds an event and deletes nothing. An explicit "that's wrong" writes a rejection with a reason, and the lesson leaves the pack on the next render. A later correction lowers the weight. Unreinforced preferences fade. Skills live in git inside the store, so a promoted skill change is one commit linked to its proposal, and reverting it is a git revert plus a ledger note. Every pack line and skill rule links to its proposal, and every proposal to its evidence, so a wrong lesson can be traced to the event that caused it.

22.5 Hill climbing, and why held-out matters#

A candidate is the current setup with one change: a reworded or new taste rule, a skill patch, a retrieval budget, a threshold, or which approved model handles a task class. The current setup is the incumbent. Today's climb builds two kinds of candidate: add one held-back lesson, or drop one auto-promoted lesson. The other kinds are designed.

The eval set has three parts, all per person: questions about the person with known answers, preference pairs (context, rejected reply, chosen reply), and replays of real past turns, cut right before the person's verdict, with what the person actually did as the target. Each part is split by time. The oldest part is train: the reviewer may read it. The middle is dev: candidates are scored on it. The newest, plus a fixed 20% sample, is held out: the loop never reads it, and it is used only for the final keep-or-drop.

The climb is greedy and conservative:

  1. Score the incumbent on dev and held out, and measure its run-to-run noise by scoring it more than once.
  2. Score the candidate on dev. Drop it unless it beats the incumbent by a margin larger than that noise.
  3. Score it on held out. Keep it only if held out does not drop and no domain drops by more than a set amount.
  4. A kept candidate becomes the new incumbent. The decision and both scores go to the ledger.

Why the held-out step matters: any process that tries many small changes and keeps the ones that score better will, given enough tries, find changes that fit the quirks of the test rather than the person. Dev alone cannot detect this, because dev is what is being fitted. Held-out data that the process never saw is the only honest witness. If a change helps on dev and not on held out, it was fitting dev. Held-out items age into dev over time and fresh ones replace them, so the held-out set keeps testing recent behaviour. And if live corrections rise while eval scores rise, the eval is stale or being gamed, which triggers a refresh.

Scoring uses two kinds of grader. Checkable rules come from the person's hard rules (for example "no em dashes" or "every approval request carries a link") and are exact. Breaking one fails the item. A judge model, from a different family than the model being tested, scores everything else over several samples, checking stance and conclusions, not only voice. Before any skill change or high-impact taste rule is promoted, it must also pass regression gates: no held-out drop, no item that passed before now failing a checkable rule, no regression at all in guarded domains, and every pinned must-pass case passing. If the person explicitly ruled the lesson, it is promoted anyway (the person outranks the eval) and the failing items are flagged as possibly stale.

What exists today in exo.eval built: loading eval sets, question-and-answer runs with pluggable responders and graders, exact, keyword, rule and judge graders, preference-choice runs with randomized order, replays built from the ledger, the time split, the noise band, comparison, the regression gate, the climb and a scoreboard, longitudinal metrics from the ledger (corrections per accepted output, verdict mix, questions asked) and HTML reports. With no held-out cases, a lesson that needs the gate fails it and says why: nothing can be proved yet. No eval scores are reported in this paper.

22.6 Cost#

The reviewer skips turns with no new signal. Classification runs on rules or a small local model. Only survivors reach a larger model, in batches. The fast loop runs only pinned cases and checkable rules for the touched domain, mostly with no model calls. The full eval and the climb run at night on an idle machine, capped at 10 candidates and 3 judge samples per item by default, under a monthly ceiling the person sets. Exo reports cost per accepted lesson. Every stage has a rules-only fallback, so the loop runs, more weakly, with no model at all.

23 Trace, findings and re-verification#

Status. built: the events, the trace reader, the rules, the findings table and its life cycle, cost per call and per turn, and the commands below.

A harness can fail in ways its own agent cannot see: a slow provider, a quiet fallback, a model shown on screen that is not the one that answered, a tool that keeps erroring. Exo treats its own behaviour as data, in one loop of four steps: record, read, report, verify.

Record. Four kinds of record go into the same numbered event stream as everything else:

  • model.call, once per model call: the role and route asked for, the route that served, every attempt with its outcome (ok, timeout, error, fallback) and time, the time to first token, the total time, the tokens including cache reads and writes, the estimated cost, and the retries.
  • tool.completed carries the duration and whether the tool returned an error.
  • turn.completed carries a timing summary: wall time, model time, tool time, time spent waiting for approvals, the number of calls, fallbacks and tool errors.
  • harness.error, for any exception the harness caught in a hook, handler, plugin, memory call or client. Catching an error is allowed. Hiding it is not.

Keys, auth headers and token-like strings are removed before any of this is written.

Read. exo trace turns a thread into one row per turn: wall time, the model that served, calls, fallbacks, tools, tool errors, tokens, cost and flags. With --calls it lists every call too. Subagent turns roll up into the parent's turn. The same table is available in a session as /trace, and to the agent as the exo_trace tool, so the agent can judge its own environment from numbers instead of impressions.

Report. At the end of each turn, a set of rules reads the records, with no model call, and files findings. Each rule has a stable signature, so a problem that repeats raises a count instead of a new row. Limits live in config, and a seat can override any of them.

SignatureFires when
turn.slowthe harness's own overhead in a turn (wall time minus tool time, approval waits and model-call time) is over 30 s
model.slow:<route>one model call took more than 60 s in total
turn.wallthe whole turn, work included, is over 30 minutes
model.wait:<route>the first token took more than 30 s
model.fallback:<route>, model.timeout:<route>a try failed and the call moved on
tool.error:<tool>:<kind>a tool returned an error
harness.error:<where>:<type>the harness caught an exception
shell.masked_exitan earlier stage of a shell pipeline failed but the reported exit was 0
tool.repeat:<tool>the same call ran twice in one turn
subagent.slowa subagent task took more than 90 s
model.mismatchthe model shown is not the one that served, and no fallback explains it

The first rule shows why the rules must measure the right thing. Wall time alone would blame the harness for a ten-minute build or a person who took a while to approve. So turn.slow subtracts tool time, approval waits and model time, and counts only what the harness itself spent; a slow provider is flagged on its route by model.slow. A separate, much higher cap catches turns that are long for any reason.

Findings come from three sources. The rules file them. The agent files them with exo_harness_report when it notices something the rules do not. And when the principal rejects an answer, or says what it should have been, that verdict files one too, while the learning loop still learns from it. All findings live in a table in the same database as the threads, so a copy of the database carries its findings with the sessions that caused them.

Verify. A finding is open until someone marks it fixed in a given commit. Then Exo keeps measuring. The same signature on a harness built from that commit or a later one reopens it. On an older harness it only counts, because the fix was not there yet. Twenty quiet turns or three days on a fixed harness mark it verified. "At or after the commit" is a git ancestry check on the source tree.

This is the harness's version of the held-out test in section 22.5. A fix is a claim about the future, and only later behaviour can confirm it. The status line of each client shows the serving route and the count of open findings, so a regression is visible without asking.

24 One surface#

Status. built: a terminal client (exo tui, written with Textual), a web client served by the command-centre plugin (exo web), a plain line client (exo chat), the ui tool and the ui.panel event. designed, not built: a device client, and decision-model choice of when to offer a panel.

Exo ships one surface instead of a command-line tool plus a separate app. The base is a terminal-like prompt and transcript. Three prefixes and one palette get you started: / for commands, @ to attach a file, ! for a shell command, and ctrl+p for a fuzzy palette of everything else. Each tool call is one line with its elapsed time, and its full output is one key away.

Richer elements are panels: a diff, a table, a task board, a choice, an approval card, a taste card, a file, a chart. The model opens one with the ui tool, which emits a ui.panel event. Each client draws what it can, and every panel carries a plain-text version, so a client that cannot draw it (a phone notice, a voice device) still says the same thing. The person can always open or close a panel by hand. What they did comes back through ui.respond (opened, dismissed, acted, ignored), and feeds the learning loop, because an ignored panel teaches as much as an opened one. Subagents and scheduled runs get no ui tool: nobody is watching them.

Instruments, not chrome. Every line on screen reports something. A status strip shows the model that actually served (and, in the signal colour, that it fell back from the one asked for), how full the context is, how much of the last call came from the provider's cache, the cost, and the number of open findings. 25 Provenance and selective upstream rebasing#

Status. built, with tests: PROVENANCE.toml, the consistency check, and exo upstream report, mark, check and merge. A scheduler now exists, but the weekly report is not yet set up as a job.

Exo learned most from Hermes Agent, an MIT-licensed harness. Three ways of using it were weighed:

  • Depend on it. Exo would run on pinned Hermes releases. But upstream changes very fast (about 490 commits a day at the time of measurement), the files Exo would patch are among its busiest, and any dependency limits how Exo can evolve.
  • Fork it. Exo would own the whole codebase: about 926,000 lines of non-test Python, plus a large TypeScript interface. Merging upstream works for a few weeks, then stops. And it cannot shrink onto a small device.
  • Lift on purpose. Build a small core, copy in the few modules that are small, clean and proven, rewrite the rest small using Hermes as the reference for edge cases, and keep pulling in upstream fixes one at a time, by choice.

Exo chose the third. In an internal weighted scoring of these options (freedom to evolve, iteration speed, reuse, device fit, coverage, upstream flow, licence, clean interface boundary), it scored 83 of 100 against 60 for a fork, 60 for a new TypeScript core and 47 for building on a closed agent SDK.

The mechanics:

  • Every lifted file starts with a header naming the upstream path, commit and licence. Hermes's MIT notice ships in LICENSES/.
  • PROVENANCE.toml has one entry per lifted or ported Exo file: its upstream paths, the mode (lift: copied then edited, port: rewritten from it, watch: upstream areas to hear about), the last upstream commit reconciled, and what changed. Today it records nine modules (the slots, the summarizer prompt, the transport base, both transports, the router, the fuzzy matcher, the patch parser and the shell runner) and two watch areas.
  • A test fails the build if a file has a lifted header without an entry, or an entry points at a file that does not exist.
  • exo upstream report keeps a fetch-only mirror of upstream, diffs each entry's upstream paths since its recorded commit (following renames, since upstream splits files often), drops noise (test-only, docs-only, bot merges), ranks the rest (fixes and security first, then features, then refactors, with commits that touch code Exo actually lifted ranked higher) and writes a report. For lifted files it also tries the upstream patch against Exo's copy and marks the clean ones.
  • exo upstream mark moves an entry's commit forward after a port, or records a refusal with a reason, so a refused change does not come back next week.

A large upstream improvement shows up as a cluster of high-ranked commits on one module. That is the signal to schedule a deliberate port.

26 Privacy and security model#

Local first. The store, ledger, agent log and threads are files on the person's machine. Embeddings are planned to run locally, and a local decision model (Laya) is the fallback when no hosted one is set. Nothing in the store is pushed anywhere without the person's explicit approval each time.

Privacy tiers. The store separates curated files (pack, router, nodes, policies), which may be committed to the store's own local git repository, from private files that are never committed: the raw corpus (directory mode 0700), the ledger, the items table and all derived indexes. Lint scans curated files for leak patterns. Importers redact secrets before anything is written.

Mechanism separate from person. The Exo package never contains store data, only templates. A test scans the new core for owner names and accounts and fails on a hit.

Content is data. Instructions come only from the person's channel. Text from tools, web pages, email and imported history is labelled as data, the summarizer is told so, and the learning loop never learns from it. Knowing the person well makes an injected instruction look out of place, but a persona is not a security boundary on its own, which is why the gate and the operator floor sit outside it.

Gates and consent. Reversible, private actions run and are logged. Irreversible, public, financial or third-party actions wait for the person. The operator floor and the credentials floor cannot be lowered by the person's own policy, and a decision model can only tighten. An approval of an outward act covers its exact text only. Standing grants are scoped, expire and are the principal's alone. A seat that approves must sign. Peers and other ingress sources can never approve.

Outbound scrub. Public posts are swept for internal names, home paths, model ids and internal ids before they leave, and held on a hit. Shell commands run without provider keys or token-like variables in their environment. Trace records have keys and token-like strings removed before they are written.

Integrity. The ledger is signed and chained, append-only is enforced by database triggers, and keys sit in 0600 files. Signatures mean trust need not rest on whoever hosts the files.

Transport. The Unix socket is owner-only. TCP requires a bearer token compared in constant time. TCP has no TLS today, so it is for localhost or a trusted network. Provider keys come from environment variables, the operating system's keychain or one owner-only key file, never from chat or from shared config. Peer messages are signed by the sending seat and checked for age and replay.

Known limits. Privacy covers what a model can infer from text, not only what is stored, and auditing outbound text for inferable facts is unsolved. Hosted model providers see what is sent to them. Exo can choose what to send, not what they keep.

27 Portability toward a companion device#

The long-term aim is a small portable device, something like a pocket companion, that carries a person's store and grows with them. The near-term design rules that keep that path open:

  • The store is one folder. Pack, nodes, items, ledger and indexes are plain files and SQLite. Copy the folder and the person moves.
  • The core stays small. Under 15,000 lines (14,694 today), enforced by a test. Two runtime dependencies. The terminal client is Python too, so the core and its main client need no Node, no browser engine and no containers. One optional tool, code_find, needs Node and hides itself when Node is missing. The planned idle memory target is under 80 MB not measured.
  • A small-model path from day one. The OpenAI-compatible transport already talks to local servers (llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio, vLLM), and routing can send routine calls to a local model.
  • Every learning stage has a rules fallback. Proposals can queue on the device and be processed later by a larger model when one is reachable.
  • The protocol suits a weak link. Numbered, replayable events, a capability handshake, and questions as events that can be answered by a button or by voice.

Wave 5 audits footprint, runs the core on a Raspberry Pi class board with an empty store and with a real store, and adds a thin device client designed, not built.

A second, longer path starts in the same place. Exo OS is a design branch that asks what an operating system looks like when an agent like this is its foundation, not an app on top. It runs the same core and the same protocol, with no fork, and lives in its own repository. It is at the research and design stage: nothing of it is built, and no machine runs it.

28 Walkthrough: one turn through every layer#

Suppose the person types, in a terminal client: "Fix the typo in README.md and push the branch." Here is that turn, layer by layer, with the actual protocol names. Steps that depend on unbuilt parts are marked.

  1. Client to core. The client sends a turn.send command with the session id, the text and mode: send. The dispatcher decodes it strictly and hands it to the engine, which starts the turn in the background and answers at once with a response {"accepted": true}.
  2. The record begins. The loop repairs any dangling tool round, stores the user message, and emits message.completed and then turn.started. The hub numbers each event, writes it to sessions.sqlite, then sends it. A web client open on the same thread sees the same events with the same numbers. The message also takes the next position in the agent's log, and the memory plugin writes it to the signed ledger as an utterance.
  3. Memory. The loop asks the memory slot for its pack block and for recall on "fix the typo in README.md and push". The pack says, among other things, that pushing is public and needs approval, and that commit messages are short. Recall goes just before the user message, not into the system message. (The staged retrieval pipeline of section 21 is not built. Recall today is keyword search.)
  4. Context. The context engine schedules leaf summaries for any full chunk of 8 log entries older than the tail, waits for pending live summaries to settle, and builds the input: the system message with the agent's summary tree, this thread's recent messages verbatim, and a note of anything other windows of the same agent did since this one last looked. If a summary finishes during this, a memory.summarized event reports the token counts before and after.
  5. Model. The loop streams from the main role. The router picks the first route whose credential is not cooling down. Suppose the first provider answers 429 before sending anything: the router records the cooldown and moves to the next route without the person seeing a thing. The model.call event records both attempts, so the fallback is not hidden, and the status strip shows the model that served. Text streams as message.delta events. The model asks for edit_file. The router prices the usage (or marks it unknown), and the loop emits usage.updated and then message.completed for the assistant message.
  6. First tool. The loop emits tool.started (risk medium). The engine runs the registry, which fires pre_tool_call. The gate classifies a local file edit as reversible and private, so it allows and logs. The fuzzy matcher finds the typo even though the model's quoted line has different spacing. The loop emits tool.completed with the duration, and stores the result as a tool message (message.completed).
  7. Second round. A fresh input is built and streamed. The model asks for bash with git commit -am "Fix README typo" && git push. tool.started goes out with risk high.
  8. The gate. The gate splits the command at &&, sees git push to a shared remote, and classifies it as public. Policy says ask. The engine emits approval.requested with a summary ("push branch to origin"), the risk, the call, the binding hash of the exact command, and who may answer (here, only the principal). The terminal shows an approval panel. A phone client could show a card. The person presses allow. The client sends approval.respond. The engine emits approval.resolved and fires post_approval_response, and the gate writes the decision to the signed ledger. Had the person chosen "always", the grant would cover this exact command to this one remote for 7 days, not every push.
  9. Shell. The shell tool runs with a timeout in its own process group, streaming output as tool.output events, then tool.completed.
  10. End of turn. The model writes a short final reply. The loop emits turn.completed with stop reason end, total usage and the timing summary, tells the memory slot about the exchange, and emits session.updated. The trace rules read the turn. The fallback in step 5 files or counts a model.fallback finding, with no model call.
  11. After the turn. Suppose that, earlier in this session, the person had rewritten one of the agent's commit messages to be shorter. That edit is a verdict: edit in the ledger. The reviewer wakes, classifies it as taste in the domain of writing, finds an existing medium-weight belief "commit messages are short and imperative", and proposes reinforcing it with this evidence. The new evidence comes from a second session, the rule is unguarded, and the weight crosses 0.75, so it is promoted and shown inline as a taste row with a link to its file. Had it been a skill change, it would have waited for the climb and been kept only if it beat the incumbent on held-out cases. (Listing it in the next brief is not built.)

If the network drops at step 7, the terminal reconnects with events.subscribe and since set to the last seq it saw, receives every event it missed, and the approval request is still open for it to answer.

32 Open questions#

  1. Spend. The monthly ceiling for the nightly eval and climb, and for hosted decision models, is the person's call and is not set by default beyond "small".
  2. The judge. The judge is the main route by default. Whether a judge from a different model family would be fairer, and how many samples make a score stable enough for one person's small eval set. Small sets are noisy. The noise band in section 22.5 may reject most changes until the set grows.
  3. Auto-promotion. Whether unguarded taste with two sessions of evidence should promote automatically (the current default) or wait for review in the brief.
  4. Cold start. How a new user's empty eval set is seeded without leaking anyone else's data, and how soon the climb can run safely.
  5. Decision models. Whether a fine-tuned small model beats rules and hosted models on real decisions, and how to calibrate with few labels. Unmeasured.
  6. Embedding model. EmbeddingGemma's licence terms versus Qwen3-Embedding's Apache 2.0, and quality on mixed-language text. Unmeasured.
  7. Terminal client stack. Wave 3 chose Python (Textual): one language and one install with the core, and no Node on the device path. The protocol keeps this reversible. If cold start on a low-power machine exceeds 1.5 seconds, or streaming a 2,000-message transcript stutters, a TypeScript renderer gets a spike.
  8. Backup. Local only, or an encrypted copy on a private remote.
  9. Inference privacy. How to audit outbound text for facts a model could infer, not only facts that are stated. Unsolved.
  10. Weights. When, if ever, to move taste from the pack into a per-person adapter. The ledger is kept in a training-ready form so this stays possible.
  11. Cache under one memory. Notes from other threads change the recent part of a call, which costs some prompt-cache reuse. On a 20-turn script with an ideal cache, prefix reuse fell from 93% to 84%. The live figure on the current code has not been measured.

33 Related work and acknowledgements#

Exo stands on other people's work. The ideas below shaped it. Any errors in applying them are ours.

Gwern Branwen, "Guardian Angels: LLM Personalization for Productivity and Security". This essay, and the reading it links to, shaped many of Exo's principles: an append-only log as the core data structure, asking when uncertain, treating the person's attention as the scarcest resource, corrections that stick, and evaluating on one person over time. Exo is one modest attempt to build part of that picture as working software. It does not claim to realize the essay's vision, and where Exo departs from it (for example on cost, local-first storage, and learning in prompts before weights) the choice is ours.

Hermes Agent (Nous Research, MIT). The harness Exo learned the most from, and the source of every lifted module recorded in PROVENANCE.toml: the memory and context slot interfaces, the transport base and message conversion, the fuzzy matcher, the patch parser, the shell runner's process-group handling and the summarizer prompt. Its background review of memory and skills is one of the two ideas fused into Exo's learning loop.

opencode. For showing how simple a coding agent can feel: one background server with thin clients, three input prefixes and a palette, and terminal preferences kept apart from project settings.

Claude Code (Anthropic). For messages between live sessions, where one running session tells another what it is doing so they do not collide. Exo's peers (section 12.7) extend that idea to Exo and Hermes agents and route it through one consent-checked ingress path.

SYM-2P (the Protean agent message language). For the line format of Exo's agent wire: one packet per line, fields by position, numeric codes and a symbolic body. Exo uses it as a checkable machine record and keeps English for models, for the measured reasons in section 16.1.

jevgrep (dzhng, MIT). For the code_find tool, which runs it, and the skill text adapted from its own.

Textual. For the framework the terminal client is built on.

Command Code. For taste learning: treating every accept, reject and edit as a signal, and showing learned taste inline as plain, editable rules with a confidence. The second idea fused into Exo's learning loop.

OptChat (Victor Taelin) and UniiChat (Higher Order Company). For the context model: one continuing conversation over a verbatim log with a tree of summaries, a fresh model call each turn, and waiting for the summaries to catch up. Exo reimplements the ideas and copies no code or prompt text.

Letta. For memory design, including memory blocks and consolidation during idle time.

Lisanne Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation" (1983). For the warning that people cannot monitor a quiet, mostly automatic system, which is why Exo gates by consequence instead of asking about every step.

Stephane Ross, Geoffrey Gordon and J. Andrew Bagnell, DAgger (2011). For the result that corrections gathered on the learner's own attempts are what make imitation learning work, which is why a verdict on the agent's own output is Exo's strongest signal.

Dylan Hadfield-Menell and colleagues, "Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning" (2016). For framing assistance as a game in which the assistant should ask and the person should teach.

Burr Settles, "Active Learning Literature Survey". For choosing questions by how much the answer would change, which shapes Exo's question queue.

Norbert Wiener, "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation" (1960). For the case that the purpose of an irreversible act must be confirmed before it is launched.

Ben Krause and colleagues, on dynamic evaluation. For the idea that weights can serve as memory, which is why the ledger is kept in a form that could train a per-person adapter later.

Stephen Robertson and colleagues (BM25), and Gordon Cormack, Charles Clarke and Stefan Buettcher (reciprocal rank fusion, 2009). For the ranking methods in Exo's retrieval design.

DSPy. For showing that prompts can be searched and improved against a metric. Exo's climb does the same with one readable change at a time.

Decision and embedding models. TypeSafe (Jev), Convai Innovations (Laya), Google (EmbeddingGemma) and the Qwen team, whose public documentation informed sections 14 and 21.

Exo's own earlier knowledge-base, ingest and team-memory systems contributed the store format, the router discipline, the item layer and the event-sourced team state described in section 19.

34 Glossary#

  • Agent. One configured Exo assistant, defined by a seat file. It has one log and one memory.
  • Append-only. Data that is only ever added to, never changed or deleted.
  • Background job. A shell command that runs on after the tool call returns, and reports back when done.
  • Binding. The hash of an outward act's exact text, target and arguments. An approval covers it only.
  • BM25. A standard keyword ranking that favours documents sharing rare query words, with diminishing returns for repeats and no bonus for length.
  • Calibration. Adjusting a model's probabilities so that "90% sure" is right about 90% of the time.
  • Candidate / incumbent. In the climb, a candidate is the current setup with one change. The incumbent is the current setup.
  • Client. A program that shows Exo to a person (terminal, web page, device) and talks to exod.
  • Context window. The text a model can read in one request.
  • Daemon. A program that runs in the background and waits for requests. exod is Exo's.
  • Decision model. A model that answers a fixed question with probabilities over fixed options instead of writing text.
  • Embedding. A list of numbers that stands for a text, arranged so that similar meanings sit close together.
  • Event. A numbered record of something that happened, sent from exod to clients and saved first.
  • Fallback. Trying the next model route when one fails before producing output.
  • Finding. A problem the harness noticed in its own behaviour, with a stable signature, a count and a status (open, fixed, verified, reopened).
  • Grant. A standing "always allow", scoped to one kind of act and target, expiring, and only the principal's to give.
  • Hash. A short fingerprint of data that changes completely if the data changes at all.
  • Hash chain. A sequence of records where each contains the hash of the one before, so tampering breaks the chain.
  • Held-out set. Test cases the learning process never sees, used only for the final keep-or-drop.
  • Hill climbing. Improving step by step, keeping a change only if it scores better.
  • Hook. A named point where a plugin can watch or change what the core is doing.
  • Ingress. The one path for input that does not come from the principal's keyboard: policy, delivery, receipt.
  • Ledger. Exo's signed, hash-chained, append-only record of events.
  • Noisy-or. A way to combine evidence: one minus the product of (one minus each strength), read as the chance that at least one piece of evidence holds.
  • Pack. The one page about the person that is loaded on every request.
  • Peer. Another live agent session on the same machine that can send messages, but never consent.
  • Principal. The person Exo works for.
  • Principal store. The folder holding everything Exo knows about one principal.
  • Prompt cache. A provider's discount for the part of a request that repeats a recent one byte for byte.
  • Proposal. A typed lesson with evidence and confidence, waiting to be promoted or dropped.
  • Provenance. The recorded origin of a piece of code or data.
  • Reciprocal rank fusion. Merging ranked lists by summing 1 divided by (60 plus the rank) across lists.
  • Retrieval. Choosing which stored notes to show the model for a given request.
  • Seat. The file that configures one agent: who it serves, its models, tools, skills and plugins.
  • Server-Sent Events (SSE). A way for a server to stream events to a client over one long HTTP response.
  • Signature. A code made with a secret key that proves who wrote a record and that it was not changed.
  • Slot. An exclusive plugin seat. Only one memory provider and one context engine can be active.
  • Subagent. A child thread started by delegate, with fewer tools, hard limits and its own model tier.
  • Summary tree. Older messages summarized in leaves of 8, merged pairwise into higher levels as needed.
  • Thread. One window, channel, scheduled run or conversation of an agent. All threads share the agent's memory.
  • Tier. One of three model roles (fast, standard, deep), cheapest to most capable.
  • Token. A piece of a word. The unit models read and are billed by, about four characters in English.
  • Trace. Exo's record of its own calls, timings, errors and costs, read by rules that file findings.
  • Verbatim tail. The most recent messages, sent to the model word for word.
  • View. Anything computed from the ledger that can be rebuilt from it.
